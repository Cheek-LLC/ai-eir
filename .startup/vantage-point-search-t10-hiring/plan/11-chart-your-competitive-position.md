# Step 11: Chart Your Competitive Position

## Real, named alternatives (not "no competitors")

| Alternative | What they actually offer | Why beachhead clients don't default to them |
|---|---|---|
| DIY (founder/CTO sources it themselves) | Free in dollars, expensive in time | This is what every one of Jordan's 6 real clients tried first — it's the real baseline, not a hypothetical |
| Large retained-search firms (Heidrick & Struggles, Spencer Stuart, Riviera Partners) | Brand, bench depth, C-level relationships | Typically decline searches below ~$150K in fees; a VP/Director-level search at a 100-person company is below their minimum |
| Generalist contingency recruiters | Fast, lower fee (10-15%), large candidate volume | Lack technical fluency to credibly screen VP-Engineering-level architecture/scaling judgment; several of Jordan's clients name this explicitly as why they moved on |
| In-network hire via VC talent partner intro (no paid search) | Free, high-trust | Works sometimes — when it does, Jordan never gets the engagement, and this is a real, ongoing source of lost deals, not just a theoretical competitor |

## Positioning axes

Charted on **technical screening depth** (x-axis) vs. **fee/cost** (y-axis): large firms sit
high-cost/high-depth; contingency recruiters sit low-cost/low-depth; Jordan positions
mid-cost/high-depth — the gap identified in Step 2's beachhead rationale, shown visually.

## Honest competitive-blind-spot check

The VC-talent-partner-intro path (free, in-network) is a real and probably under-acknowledged
competitor — it doesn't show up as a "competitor" in the traditional sense because no company is
selling it, but it wins deals Jordan never even hears about. Naming this explicitly rather than
omitting it because it doesn't fit neatly as a company-shaped competitor.
